## Abstract

Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems are sold to users on the premise that inline citations make their outputs verifiable. I argue that citation infidelity, where citations point to retrieved documents that do not actually support the cited claim, is a distinct trust failure. It is worse than no citations at all, because it manufactures false confidence. Before running anything I pre-registered a metric (Citation Support Rate, or CSR), five hard cases, and three verifier interventions of increasing cost: Chain-of-Verification, an off-the-shelf NLI verifier, and a quote-then-cite re-prompt. The evaluation set is 50 multi-hop questions from HotpotQA plus the 5 hard cases, with 10 held-out questions reserved for qualitative analysis. I report each intervention's CSR with bootstrap confidence intervals, paired comparisons against the baseline, and an explicit trust-tax analysis covering latency, tokens, and abstention. Numbers are filled in after the locked evaluation.

## 1. Introduction

In June 2023, the Southern District of New York sanctioned two attorneys $5,000 each for filing a brief that cited fabricated cases generated by ChatGPT. The case is *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.*, 678 F. Supp. 3d 443 (S.D.N.Y. 2023). The attorneys were caught not because the model's prose was poor, since it was actually fluent, but because the cited cases did not exist. Citations turned a fluent hallucination into a sanctionable filing.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is the dominant industry response to this kind of failure. Ground the model in retrieved documents, and require it to cite them. The trouble is that grounding the retrieval does not by itself ground the citation. A RAG system can retrieve the right documents and still attach citations to claims those documents do not support. Recent benchmarks confirm the gap. Even strong models on the ELI5 dataset lack complete citation support roughly half the time (Gao et al., 2023).

I focus on this specific failure mode for three reasons. First, it is the failure most likely to deceive users, because citations function as a verification shortcut. A reader who trusts the citation skips the verification step. Second, it is operationalizable. Given a claim and a cited chunk, "does the chunk support the claim?" is a well-defined judgment (Rashkin et al., 2023). Third, fixes can be tested without retraining the underlying model, which fits a one-week project budget.

The contributions of this paper are:

1. A pre-registered metric (Citation Support Rate), five hard cases, and concrete failure predictions for a baseline RAG system.
2. Implementation and evaluation of three verifier interventions: a citation-grounded variant of Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala et al., 2024), an off-the-shelf NLI verifier, and a quote-then-cite re-prompt inspired by Self-RAG's reflection step (Asai et al., 2024).
3. A trust-tax analysis covering latency, tokens, and abstention rate, plus a faithfulness-versus-cost Pareto plot, so that the practical cost of each intervention sits next to its faithfulness gain rather than getting buried in an appendix.

## 2. Related Work

Hallucination in natural language generation is a long-running problem; Ji et al. (2023) is the canonical survey. The Attributable to Identified Sources (AIS) framework of Rashkin et al. (2023) treats attribution as a binary judgment about whether a claim is verifiable from a provided source, and it is the conceptual ancestor of the metric I use here. FActScore (Min et al., 2023) scaled this idea to long-form generation by decomposing outputs into atomic facts and scoring each one with retrieval plus an LLM judge. I borrow that atomic-claim decomposition. The most direct prior benchmark is ALCE (Gao et al., 2023), which evaluates LLM citations along three axes: fluency, correctness, and citation quality.

On the intervention side, Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala et al., 2024) reduces hallucination through a four-step process. The model drafts an answer, generates verification questions about its own draft, answers those questions independently, and then revises. Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2024) trains a model to emit reflection tokens that gate retrieval and self-critique, but it requires fine-tuning. My quote-then-cite intervention is a prompt-only approximation of Self-RAG's "is this supported?" step, intended to test whether you can get most of the benefit without training.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 System Under Test

The baseline pipeline retrieves the top-5 paragraphs from a fixed Wikipedia corpus assembled from HotpotQA distractor contexts (Yang et al., 2018), using BM25 over whitespace tokens. The generator (`claude-haiku-4-5`) is prompted to produce an answer with inline citations of the form `[doc_id]` after every factual claim, and to abstain if the sources do not answer the question. The exact system prompt and configuration are committed to git. See `rag_pipeline.py:BASELINE_SYS`.

### 3.2 Trust Metric

For each generated answer I segment the text into sentence-level cited claims. For every (claim, cited_chunk) pair, an independent LLM judge (`claude-opus-4-7`) returns one of {SUPPORTED, PARTIAL, UNSUPPORTED, CONTRADICTED}. CSR is the fraction of pairs judged SUPPORTED:

$$\text{CSR} = \frac{|\{(c,d) : \text{judge}(c,d) = \text{SUPPORTED}\}|}{|\{(c,d)\}|}.$$

A claim cited to a `doc_id` that is not present in the retrieved set is counted as UNSUPPORTED. This captures hallucinated `doc_id`s, and I also report it separately as a "citation precision" metric so the failure mode does not get hidden in the aggregate.

**Judge validation.** Before the locked run, I hand-labeled 30 pilot pairs and computed Cohen's kappa between the human and automated judge. The pre-registration requires kappa of at least 0.6 on the binary collapse (SUPPORTED versus everything else) before the prompt is locked. Result: kappa = [fill in].

### 3.3 Interventions

**A. Chain-of-Verification (CoVe), citation-grounded.** Following Dhuliawala et al. (2024), the system (i) takes the baseline draft, (ii) prompts the model to decompose the draft into atomic claims with their cited `doc_id`s, (iii) for each claim asks the model whether the cited chunk literally supports it, and (iv) drops unsupported claims. If nothing remains after dropping, the system abstains.

**B. NLI verifier.** A `microsoft/deberta-v3-large-mnli` classifier scores each (chunk, claim) pair, and any claim with $P(\text{ENTAILMENT}) < 0.5$ is dropped. This is the only intervention that does not add an LLM call per claim, which makes it the obvious cheap baseline.

**C. Quote-then-cite re-prompt.** A single follow-up call asks the model to find a verbatim span of at most 25 words in the cited chunk for every claim, and to drop any claim for which it cannot. One extra call total, regardless of how many claims there are.

### 3.4 Pre-Registered Hard Cases

Five cases written before any runs: multi-hop numeric, negation, entity collision, temporal staleness, and aggregation across documents. Full text is in `preregistration.md` section 4. I predicted the baseline would fail on at least three.

### 3.5 Statistical Analysis

CSR is reported as a question-level mean with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, using 10,000 resamples. Each intervention is compared to the baseline by paired bootstrap on the subset of questions where both produced citations (questions where one mode abstained are excluded from the paired comparison, since a missing CSR cannot be paired against a real one).

## 7. Limitations and Discussion

**Faithfulness is not truth.** CSR measures fidelity to retrieved text, not to ground truth. If the corpus is wrong, an intervention that maximizes CSR will faithfully propagate wrongness. For domains where the corpus is itself noisy, this is the most important caveat in the paper.

**Single corpus, single language, single retriever.** The evaluation uses a Wikipedia-derived corpus, English questions, and BM25. A dense retriever or a noisier corpus may shift the failure modes that the interventions catch. I would not generalize the rank-ordering of interventions to a legal corpus without re-running.

**Judge bias.** The judge is itself an LLM. LLM judges are known to favor verbose or hedged answers. I mitigate by using a different model family for the judge versus the generator, by validating against human-labeled pairs, and by reporting Cohen's kappa rather than only raw agreement.

**Self-verification by the same model.** CoVe and quote-then-cite both use the same model to verify its own draft. If the model is systematically miscalibrated about a claim, it will verify that claim confidently, and the intervention will pass it through. The NLI verifier is the only one immune to this failure mode, which makes its result especially informative even if its absolute CSR is lower.

**Who remains unprotected.** Users who never click citations. Users in domains where the corpus is incomplete or stale. Users in non-English languages. None of these interventions help these populations directly, and the report should not pretend otherwise.
